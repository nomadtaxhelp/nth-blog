A developer spends four months in one country, three in another and the rest visiting family and clients. As far as the developer can see, no country has a majority of the year. A tax authority can ask a different question: *where was your home when you weren't travelling?* Depending on the country, an available home, a resident partner, where you work or your residence in earlier years can change the result.

There is no worldwide register that assigns each person exactly one tax residence. Countries apply their own laws, so one country, several countries or potentially none may treat you as resident for a given period. Start with the place that taxed you last year. Establish what changed there, then check each place where you have spent time, kept a home or carried on work. [OECD guidance and jurisdiction-specific rules](https://www.oecd.org/en/networks/global-forum-tax-transparency/resources/aeoi-implementation-portal/tax-residency.html).

## Two travel calendars with different answers

Consider two hypothetical consultants across the same UK tax year, **6 April 2025 to 5 April 2026**. Each spent exactly 60 counted days in the UK. Assume the totals below have been checked against travel records, including the UK rules for counting days. Neither has died during the year or claims split-year treatment.

| Facts to check | Consultant A | Consultant B |
|---|---|---|
| Travel calendar | 60 days UK, 120 Portugal, 95 Spain, 90 elsewhere | 60 days UK, 120 Portugal, 95 Spain, 90 elsewhere |
| Previous residence | UK resident in the preceding tax year | Non-UK resident in all three preceding tax years |
| Family and accommodation | UK-resident spouse and a UK home available and used. The facts meet both statutory tie definitions. | Partner and established home abroad. UK stays are short hotel visits and the facts give no UK family or accommodation tie. |
| Work on UK visits | 42 UK days with more than three hours’ work, meeting the UK work tie | Two short UK client meetings, with no UK work tie |
| Other assumptions | No automatic UK or overseas test applies | No automatic UK or overseas test applies |

**Consultant A is UK resident under these assumptions.** HMRC’s table for someone resident in at least one of the three previous tax years requires three ties at 46 to 90 UK days. Family, accommodation and work supply those ties. A count below six months does not settle this person's case.

**Consultant B is UK non-resident under these assumptions.** For a person non-resident in all three previous years who spends 46 to 90 days in the UK, HMRC’s table requires all four ties. Several are absent. The answer applies to the UK only. To reach an answer for Portugal, Spain and the other destinations, we would need the full list of countries, dates in their own tax years, homes, family arrangements and business facts. The 120 days shown in the UK tax year need not equal 120 days in a calendar-year jurisdiction. [HMRC’s Statutory Residence Test, section 4 and tables A and B](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/rdr3-statutory-residence-test-srt/guidance-note-for-statutory-residence-test-srt-rdr3).

## How to check your own year

1. **Rebuild your calendar in the right periods.** Start with each jurisdiction's tax year, then record arrival and departure dates, overnight locations, workdays and time worked. Keep evidence for the days close to a threshold.
2. **List every available home.** For each address, record when you had access, who lived there, when you stayed and whether another tenant had exclusive use. Keep leases and handover dates alongside the calendar.
3. **Record where your life and work actually happened.** Where did your partner or children live? From where did you deliver services or direct a business? The Netherlands, for example, considers several features of a person's life when deciding if they have emigrated. [Dutch departure guide](/blog/posts/leaving-the-netherlands-tax-residency).
4. **Resolve the country-by-country results.** Mark each country as resident, non-resident or unresolved, and write down the facts behind that conclusion. If two claims overlap, investigate the actual treaty, its scope and any relief available. For an uncertain year, give a qualified adviser the calendar and the specific point you cannot resolve.

## Even if you are non-resident, who can tax the income?

Take a contractor who has established UK non-residence but still receives rent from a UK flat. UK-source income can remain subject to UK tax. A consultancy fee presents a different question: what work generated it, who received it and where was it performed? For a company, consider its own residence, activities and filings as well. Putting an invoice through a foreign account changes none of those underlying facts. [HMRC on residence and UK-source income](https://www.gov.uk/tax-foreign-income/residence).

US citizens and resident aliens face a further issue. US federal rules generally apply to worldwide income while living abroad. Filing thresholds, credits and exclusions are separate questions. [IRS Publication 54](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p54).

## What do you tell a bank during the move?

A bank may ask for a residential address, all jurisdictions of tax residence and the corresponding tax identification numbers. A residence permit establishes permission to live somewhere. On its own, it does not show that tax residence has begun there or ended in your former country. If you genuinely believe no country treats you as resident, ask the bank how it handles that circumstance and get the analysis checked before signing.
