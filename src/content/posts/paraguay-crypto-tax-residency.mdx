Before selling a crypto portfolio after a move, separate three questions: which country can tax you, how it treats the transaction, and what you must report. A zero tax bill on a particular transaction does not necessarily mean no reporting.

The claim that a foreign exchange or a self-custody wallet automatically makes every gain foreign-source is too broad. A blockchain does not provide a simple “foreign address” for tax purposes.

## What changed in 2026?

DNIT's **General Resolution 47/2026** introduced an annual cryptoasset information return. It covers platforms operating in Paraguay and specified resident individuals and entities using foreign platforms or transacting without an intermediary when annual transactions exceed **US$5,000**, individually or in aggregate.

For taxpayers with a 31 December year-end, it applies from the 2026 fiscal year. The return is due in the third month following year-end under the relevant filing calendar, through Marangatu. Covered users must register obligation **959-DJI Criptoactivos**; those without a RUC must register. The rule covers more than cashing out, including holdings, transfers, swaps and rewards. [Read Articles 3–7 of Resolution 47/2026](https://www.dnit.gov.py/en/web/portal-institucional/w/resoluci%C3%B3n-general-dnit-n%C2%B0-47/26).

That threshold measures transactions, not simply taxable profit. Have your accountant check your actual activity against the rule rather than comparing it only with the gain you withdrew.

## Reporting and tax are different questions

The new return does not establish a blanket 0% crypto regime. Paraguay's tax law distinguishes income types and sources. The correct treatment needs to address what you do and whether you hold assets personally or operate through a business. [Paraguay's tax law](https://www.bacn.gov.py/leyes-paraguayas/9332/ley-n-6380-de-modernizaci-n-y-simplificaci-n-del-sistema-tributario-nacional).

Give the adviser a complete activity list: occasional sales, frequent trading, mining, staking, lending, liquidity pools, token compensation or other rewards. Do not carry a conclusion about one activity across to all of them.

## Moving does not erase the earlier tax position

The date on a residency card is not necessarily the date your old tax residence ends. Before a disposal, check the departure-year rules, any applicable exit taxes, continuing source-country taxes and consequences of a later return.

For example, HMRC's temporary non-residence rules can bring certain income and gains into charge when a person returns to the UK, if the conditions apply. A period abroad is not automatically a permanent escape from UK tax. [HMRC's residence guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/rdr3-statutory-residence-test-srt/guidance-note-for-statutory-residence-test-srt-rdr3).

## Build a transaction file before changing accounts

| Record | Why to keep it |
|---|---|
| Acquisition dates, quantities and costs | Reconstruct the history of the position |
| Exchange exports and wallet history | Reconcile trades, transfers and balances |
| Ownership of wallets and accounts | Distinguish your own transfers from third-party transactions |
| Fiat values and fees at transaction time | Support reporting and any tax calculation |
| Work and travel records | Establish what you were doing and where |
| Written advice and filed returns | Record the treatment actually adopted |

Export the history before closing an account. Keep access to records even if the exchange no longer serves your new residence. Update identification details accurately; moving coins or changing an IP address does not establish a new tax position.

## Questions to settle before a sale

1. On the intended sale date, where will I be tax resident under each relevant country's rules?
2. How will each transaction be classified, including swaps and rewards?
3. Does the Paraguay reporting obligation apply to my annual activity?
4. What documents will the receiving bank need for source-of-funds checks?
5. What changes if I return to my former country?
